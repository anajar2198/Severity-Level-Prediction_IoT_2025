\begin{abstract}
This paper presents an experimentally validated method for detecting false-data-injection attacks (FDIAs) and assessing attack severity in heterogeneous inverter-fed motor drives. The method combines low-frequency, high-frequency, and time-domain electrical features with an InceptionTime classifier. Motor-terminal currents and point-of-common-coupling (PCC) voltage and current are evaluated separately on an alternating current induction motor (ACIM) and a permanent magnet synchronous motor (PMSM) platform under three speed and three load settings. Training, validation, and test sets contain separate recordings and attack episodes. Across three recording partitions, binary detection achieves mean accuracies of 87.41\% with motor-terminal sensing and 80.78\% with PCC sensing. Four-class severity balanced accuracy is 90.81\% and 55.62\%, respectively. Motor-terminal sensing provides stronger severity discrimination, while PCC sensing avoids dedicated instrumentation at each motor and offers a shared monitoring point for heterogeneous drives. For PMSM-to-ACIM transfer, fine-tuning with a nominal 1\% of labeled target episodes raises PCC balanced accuracy from 56.00\% to 77.43\%, reaching 91.38\% at 20\%. Adaptation is less effective in the reverse direction. PCC feature pruning preserves 80.29\% balanced accuracy while reducing processing time from 23.03 to 12.44~ms. These results demonstrate the potential of nonintrusive PCC monitoring and limited-label adaptation on the tested platform.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{F}{DIA} detection and severity assessment are important in networked motor-drive systems, where manipulated sensor feedback can degrade control performance and cause unsafe operation~\cite{Ghaderi2021A}. Increasing drive connectivity makes timely detection of sensor-oriented cyber attacks essential for resilient operation~\cite{Yang2022Fast,Yang2024Enhanced}.
Current signals are attractive for FDIA monitoring because they are easy to acquire, strongly coupled to electromechanical dynamics, and have been shown to reveal sensor-integrity attacks in motor drives~\cite{7944701, YangVulnerability}. However, most existing studies emphasize terminal-side sensing, operate on a single machine domain, or assume sufficient labeled data for supervised training. These assumptions limit practicality in heterogeneous deployments where different motor types, sensing locations, and data availability constraints all affect performance.
Motivated by these limitations, this work experimentally evaluates FDIA detection in a heterogeneous motor-drive setting involving both ACIM and PMSM domains. The study evaluates binary detection (\texttt{fdia2}), four-class injected-bias assessment (\texttt{severity4}), cross-motor adaptation, and processing time for converter-fed industrial loads connected at a shared electrical interface. The framework combines low-frequency (LF), high-frequency (HF), and time-domain (TD) electrical features with an InceptionTime classifier. Separate models are trained for motor-terminal and PCC sensing. An auxiliary motor-type classifier (\texttt{motor2}) distinguishes ACIM and PMSM recording conditions.

\subsection{Challenges at PCC in Heterogeneous Multi-Motor Systems}
\label{sec:pcc_challenges}

From a deployment perspective, motor-terminal measurements provide cleaner machine-specific signatures, whereas PCC measurements are more scalable and non-intrusive. PCC sensing is attractive because it avoids direct motor-terminal instrumentation and is compatible with shared electrical infrastructure~\cite{7271086}. However, PCC currents are affected by converter harmonics, network interactions, and load aggregation. Consequently, PCC attack signatures are weaker and less separable than motor-terminal signatures~\cite{10005067}.
The problem becomes more difficult in heterogeneous multi-motor settings. ACIM and PMSM systems exhibit different current characteristics even under the same attack framework, which introduces a substantial domain shift between source and target deployments. Consequently, a detector trained on one motor family may generalize poorly to the other unless explicit adaptation is used. Therefore, this study evaluates in-domain performance, source-only transfer, few-shot adaptation, and transfer-direction asymmetry.
\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
The main contributions of this work are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Experimental PCC-based FDIA monitoring:} A multiscale feature and InceptionTime framework is validated using 540 attack episodes across three speeds and three load settings for each motor type. Complete recordings and their episodes are separated between training, validation, and testing, enabling evaluation on unseen recordings without temporal leakage between these sets.
\item \textbf{Detection and severity assessment at a shared sensing point:} Separate motor-terminal and PCC models quantify the sensing tradeoff. PCC monitoring detects attacks without dedicated motor-terminal instrumentation, while severity-conditioned attack recall identifies attacks even when their exact bias level is misclassified.
\item \textbf{Limited-label adaptation and reduced-feature inference:} Bidirectional cross-motor experiments show that a nominal 1\% target-episode budget improves PMSM-to-ACIM PCC balanced accuracy by 21.43 percentage points. Validation-based feature pruning preserves PCC detection performance with 18 of 74 features and an 85.18\% processing speedup.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedWorks}

FDIA detection has been studied extensively in smart grids and broader cyber-physical systems (CPS), but their system models and measurement requirements differ from those of inverter-fed motor drives. Many grid- and CPS-oriented approaches rely on state estimation, observer-based detection, invariant analysis, adaptive neuro-fuzzy models, or graph-based learning~\cite{7438916,basati2022fdi,nath2022quickest,beg2017fdia,sargolzaei2020fdia,10016905}. These studies demonstrate strong FDIA-detection capability at the system level, but they are generally not designed for drive-level current monitoring or for deployment at a shared point of common coupling (PCC).

Motor-drive-specific studies are more closely related to the present work. Prior work has shown that current signals can support effective FDIA detection in electric-drive systems and that transfer learning can improve performance when labeled data are limited~\cite{Yang2022Fast,Yang2024Enhanced}. Related industrial-IoT monitoring studies have also considered cyber-detection in machine-monitoring settings~\cite{423563247547}. However, these studies mainly rely on motor-terminal sensing, single-motor settings, or closely related operating domains, and therefore provide limited evidence for heterogeneous cross-motor transfer.


The main gap is not FDIA detection in general, which has been widely studied in smart grids and cyber-physical systems, but nonintrusive PCC-based monitoring for heterogeneous inverter-fed motor-drive systems. In particular, the existing literature does not clearly establish: 1) how PCC sensing compares with motor-side sensing for low-severity current-feedback FDIAs, 2) whether PCC measurements support reliable multi-level severity assessment rather than only binary detection, given that attack signatures become weaker and less separable under signal aggregation and harmonic distortion, and 3) how detection performance transfers between heterogeneous ACIM and PMSM domains when labeled target-domain data are limited. The emphasis of the present work is the experimental assessment of sensing location, attack magnitude, and cross-motor adaptation using a common classification framework. This work addresses these three questions experimentally on a shared ACIM/PMSM platform using multi-scale features, in-domain evaluation, source-only cross-domain transfer, and few-shot target-domain adaptation.









\section{System and Data}
\label{sec:system_data}
\subsection{Experimental Platform: ACIM/PMSM and PCC Sensing}
\label{sec:experimental_platform}

Fig.~\ref{fig:system_schematic} illustrates the overall setup, including the shared PCC, the two drive branches, the ACIM and PMSM loads, the sensing locations, and the FDIA injection points in the control/feedback paths. The experimental platform consists of a heterogeneous two-motor industrial setup including one ACIM branch and one PMSM branch. Both branches are supplied from a common upstream electrical interface, enabling measurements at the PCC in addition to motor-side measurements. This setup enables comparison of motor-terminal and PCC sensing under heterogeneous ACIM/PMSM dynamics.
The dataset contains 540 scheduled attack episodes (270 for each motor), formed by repeating 30 bias patterns over nine speed/load combinations per motor. All episodes were acquired on the same two-motor platform. The experiments cover three speed operating points, namely 900, 1100, and 1300 RPM.

Two measurement streams were used throughout this study. The first stream corresponds to motor-side measurements, referred to as \emph{motor} in the remainder of the paper. The second stream corresponds to PCC-side measurements, referred to as \emph{PCC}. The PCC stream is the primary deployment-oriented sensing location because it avoids dedicated sensors at each machine.

\subsection{Attack Model, Sensing Assumptions, and Labels}
\label{sec:attack_labels}

In the testbed, the FDIA is injected into the controller's phase-current feedback path. The PCC and motor-side monitoring sensors are used as trusted measurements to record the physical response of the drive system under attack.
The monitoring sensors are isolated from the compromised controller feedback path. Thus, PCC sensing represents the nonintrusive monitoring case, while motor-side sensing serves as a performance reference. Replay, denial-of-service, monitoring-sensor attacks, and malicious command manipulation are outside the scope of this study.

Let $\mathbf{i}_{abc}(t)=[i_a(t),i_b(t),i_c(t)]^\top$ denote the true three-phase current-feedback vector in the controller's per-unit representation, and let $\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_{abc}(t)$ denote the corrupted current-feedback vector received by the motor controller. During an attack interval, the FDIA is modeled as an additive phase-current bias:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_{abc}(t)
=
\mathbf{i}_{abc}(t)+\boldsymbol{\alpha},
\qquad t\in[t_{\mathrm{on}},t_{\mathrm{off}}],
\label{eq:fdia_model}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\alpha_a,\alpha_b,\alpha_c]^\top\) denotes the injected per-unit bias vector, with \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{0}\) outside the attack interval. The tested nonzero bias magnitudes range from \(0.0005\) to \(0.0050~\mathrm{pu}\), corresponding to \(0.05\%\)--\(0.5\%\) current-feedback bias. The bias is normalized to the nominal controller current-feedback base. In this study, severity is defined by the injected bias magnitude; physical consequences such as shaft-torque deviation are not used as severity labels. Attack severity is measured by the largest absolute injected phase-current bias,
\(s=\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_\infty\).

For binary FDIA detection, each window is labeled as
$y_{\mathrm{fdia2}}=1$ if $s>0$ and $y_{\mathrm{fdia2}}=0$ otherwise.
For four-class severity assessment, the label is defined as
\begin{equation}
y_{\mathrm{severity4}} =
\begin{cases}
0, & s=0 \quad \text{(normal)},\\
1, & 0.0005 \leq s \leq 0.0015 \quad \text{(low)},\\
2, & 0.0020 \leq s \leq 0.0035 \quad \text{(medium)},\\
3, & 0.0040 \leq s \leq 0.0050 \quad \text{(high)}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:severity_bins}
\end{equation}
Cases 21--30 have $s=0.0050$ throughout because phase A remains fixed; all belong to the high-bias class. The resulting attack-window counts are 5,400 low, 7,200 medium, and 14,400 high.
Table~\ref{tab:cyberattack_dataset_structure} summarizes the swept single-phase, equal two-phase, and fixed-plus-swept two-phase FDIA cases. Across three speeds, three load settings, and two motor types, the dataset contains 540 total attack cases. Each recording contains 30 consecutive 20-s episodes, with 10~s normal operation followed by 10~s FDIA operation. In total, the dataset contains 33,054 normal and 27,000 attack windows.

\subsection{Data Acquisition and Operating Conditions}
\label{sec:data_acquisition}

Electrical measurements were acquired using a National Instruments cDAQ-9185 chassis and a custom data-collector interface board. The board routed synchronized motor-side and PCC-side analog channels to the acquisition system. Table~\ref{tab:measurement_streams} summarizes the measured signals and channel counts. Three brake-excitation settings were tested for each machine: 1--3~V for the ACIM branch and 2--4~V for the PMSM branch. Because torque was not directly measured, these voltages are used as load-condition labels. All channels were sampled at 10~kHz and stored in TDMS format. Each recording was segmented into non-overlapping 0.2-s windows containing 2,000 samples.

To prevent temporal leakage, each complete recording and all its episodes were assigned to only one of the training, validation, or test sets. Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_protocols} summarizes the dataset dimensions and evaluation protocols. Three seeds (1234, 2234, and 3234) changed the recording assignment and model initialization. Normalization statistics were fitted using training recordings only. The test sets therefore contain unseen recordings of speed/load combinations from the same apparatus; recordings can recur across the three repeats.

\section{The Proposed Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{LF/HF Feature Extraction Pipeline}
\label{sec:lfhf_features}


To represent both slow electromechanical behavior and faster attack-induced disturbances, a two-scale LF/HF feature pipeline was applied independently to each measured channel. For every sample, a 2{,}000-point outer window was first extracted from the raw 10~kHz recording. Within this outer window, two parallel sliding analyses were performed. The LF branch uses a 500-sample window with a stride of 25 samples, while the HF branch uses a 250-sample window with a stride of 29 samples. The LF and HF settings both produce 61 time steps, enabling concatenation into one temporal feature tensor.
Fig.~\ref{fig:lfhf_motor_pcc_examples} illustrates representative motor-current and PCC-current responses around attack onset. LF and HF windows are overlaid on the waveforms, and the lower panels show the corresponding feature responses. Signal amplitudes are in recorded acquisition units.

In total, 37 features were extracted per channel, including 18 LF spectral features, 11 HF spectral/envelope features, and 8 TD features. The LF branch targets slow electromechanical changes, fundamental-frequency behavior, harmonic distortion, sideband effects, and low-band energy redistribution. The HF branch captures higher-frequency redistribution and envelope-related changes that may appear during small current-feedback FDIAs. The TD features provide complementary sensitivity to amplitude variation, impulsiveness, waveform asymmetry, and short-term signal complexity.
Together, these features represent changes in current-feedback integrity across complementary time and frequency scales, supporting detection of weak PCC signatures under harmonics and signal aggregation.

\subsection{Inception-Based Detection Model}
\label{sec:inception_model}

The extracted LF/HF tensors were modeled using a one-dimensional InceptionTime architecture~\cite{fawaz2020inceptiontime} for multivariate time-series classification. The input dimension is $(61,222)$ for the motor stream and $(61,74)$ for the PCC stream. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:inceptiontime_architecture}, the network uses bottleneck compression, parallel one-dimensional convolutional kernels of lengths 39, 19, and 9, residual shortcuts, global average pooling, and a dense output layer. Motor and PCC models used stream-specific hyperparameters selected by validation balanced accuracy. The Inception-based design is well suited to the proposed LF/HF representation because the extracted features retain temporal ordering across the 61-step sequence. This preserves temporal interactions among LF, HF, and TD features. Each task uses a separately trained model with two outputs for \texttt{fdia2} or \texttt{motor2}, and four outputs for \texttt{severity4}.

\subsection{Architecture of Online FDIA Detection and Attack-Severity Estimation}
\label{sec:AnomalyDetection}

The proposed pipeline supports online monitoring from trusted motor-terminal currents or PCC voltage and current. Fig.~\ref{fig:Online-algorithem} illustrates the measurement, feature-extraction, and inference stages. For either sensing configuration, each 2,000-sample window is converted into a 61-step multivariate LF/HF/TD feature sequence, normalized using training-set statistics, and evaluated by the corresponding model. Motor-terminal and PCC inputs are processed separately. The modular pipeline supports feature pruning, reduced-input models, and cross-motor adaptation. It provides alarms, severity indicators, and logs for supervisory systems; automatic mitigation is outside the scope of this study.

\subsection{Training Details and Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:training_details}

Hyperparameter selection was performed separately for each task and sensing stream using validation balanced accuracy. The same 100 candidate configurations were evaluated for two tasks, two sensing streams, and three recording partitions, totaling 1,200 training runs. The search varied depth (3/4/6), filters (16/24/32/48), bottleneck width (8/16/32), dropout (0.20/0.35/0.50), learning rate ($5\times10^{-4}$/$10^{-3}$), weight decay ($5\times10^{-5}$/$10^{-4}$), and batch size (64/128). Table~\ref{tab:search_top5_combined} summarizes the top-ranked severity configurations for both sensing streams across all three seeds.

Training used Adam and weighted cross-entropy, assigning larger weights to less frequent classes. Each run used up to 60 epochs, with early stopping after 10 epochs without improved validation balanced accuracy. The learning rate was halved after three unimproved epochs, down to $10^{-6}$. The best checkpoint and configuration were selected independently within each recording partition using validation balanced accuracy; ties followed the fixed candidate order. Test results were not used for selection.

Reported test metrics are mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation over the three holdouts. Precision, recall, and F1 are macro-averaged, and balanced accuracy is the mean class recall. The standard deviations describe variation across recording assignments and model initializations, rather than independent physical replications.

\subsection{Domain Adaptation Strategy: Source-Only vs Few-Shot Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:domain_adaptation}

Few-shot adaptation was evaluated by training on one motor type and testing on the other, in both ACIM $\rightarrow$ PMSM and PMSM $\rightarrow$ ACIM directions. For each direction, six source recordings were used for training and three for validation. The same 100-candidate search selected the source model using source validation balanced accuracy. Applying this model directly to the target motor gives the \emph{source-only} result and measures cross-motor domain shift.

For adaptation, the source model was fine-tuned using a small labeled subset of the target adaptation pool in Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_protocols}. The nominal budgets were 1\%, 5\%, 10\%, and 20\%, sampled as complete episode groups; the 1\% budget corresponds to two of 180 groups (1.11\%). All adaptation data remained separate from the target test recordings. Fine-tuning used 20 epochs at 0.2 times the source learning rate, with source normalization fixed. Each condition was repeated over three seeds. This supervised adaptation tests whether limited target labels can improve a detector when the deployed motor type differs from the training domain.

\subsection{In-Domain Baselines: Motor vs PCC}
\label{sec:in_domain}

Table~\ref{tab:all_tasks_in_domain} reports in-domain performance with both ACIM and PMSM recordings represented in the training, validation, and test sets. Motor-terminal sensing provides stronger class separation for both \texttt{fdia2} and \texttt{severity4}, while PCC sensing supports nonintrusive detection at the shared electrical interface. Severity assessment is more challenging than binary detection at the PCC, where the drive responses are observed through the common supply. Fig.~\ref{fig:main_confusion_matrices} shows the corresponding classwise results, using row percentages from prediction counts pooled over the three holdouts.

Exact-severity recall counts attacks assigned to the correct bias class. Severity-conditioned attack recall also counts attacks assigned to another nonzero bias class. Fig.~\ref{fig:severity_detection_decisions} presents this second view of the PCC severity model: it detects 88.1\% of high-bias attacks as some attack level, compared with 70.8\% assigned to the exact high-bias class. Thus, an incorrect severity estimate can still provide an attack indication. The same model labels 37.3\% of normal windows as attacks, so this detection benefit must be considered together with false alarms. These decisions come from the four-class model; the separately trained binary detector in Fig.~\ref{fig:main_confusion_matrices}(b) has fewer false alarms.

\subsection{Comparison with Deep and Classical ML Baselines}
\label{sec:model_comparison}

To benchmark the proposed LF/HF Inception-based model, representative deep-learning and classical ML baselines were evaluated on the PCC \texttt{fdia2} and \texttt{severity4} tasks. These are the more challenging sensing conditions and the main focus for nonintrusive deployment. ResNet and LSTM-FCN provide alternative deep temporal models; Random Forest and XGBoost provide tree-based classifiers; MultiRocket~\cite{tan2022multirocket} provides a convolutional-transform baseline. All methods use the same recording partitions and feature inputs, with candidate settings selected using validation balanced accuracy. Search budgets differ between model families.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison_hard_tasks}, InceptionTime achieves the highest mean accuracy, balanced accuracy, and macro F1 for PCC binary detection. For severity assessment, it achieves the highest mean balanced accuracy, indicating stronger average recall across the four classes. XGBoost has higher overall accuracy, while LSTM-FCN has slightly higher macro F1. The proposed representation therefore supports both PCC detection and class-balanced severity assessment, without outperforming every baseline under every metric.

\subsection{Domain Adaptation Results: 1/5/10/20\%}
\label{sec:adaptation_results}

Table~\ref{tab:domain_adaptation_anomaly2} reports \texttt{fdia2} adaptation using nominal target-label budgets of 1\%, 5\%, 10\%, and 20\%. The largest early improvement occurs for PMSM $\rightarrow$ ACIM: with only the 1\% budget, balanced accuracy rises from 36.08\% to 64.35\% for motor sensing and from 56.00\% to 77.43\% for PCC sensing. These gains of 28.27 and 21.43 percentage points show that a small number of labeled target episodes can substantially improve cross-motor detection.

At 20\%, PMSM-to-ACIM balanced accuracy reaches 92.63\% for motor sensing and 91.38\% for PCC sensing. ACIM-to-PMSM adaptation is less effective, reaching 75.99\% and 56.30\%, respectively. Thus, target-domain fine-tuning can make PCC monitoring useful beyond the source motor type, but its benefit depends strongly on transfer direction. The results support limited-label calibration for the tested PMSM-to-ACIM deployment rather than uniform transfer performance across motor types.

\section{LATENCY AND REDUCED-FEATURE INFERENCE ANALYSIS}
\label{sec:realtime}
Practical PCC monitoring requires processing each measurement window within the acquisition interval. This section evaluates processing time and throughput, then examines the effects of feature selection and LF/HF/TD feature-family reduction.

\subsection{Inference Latency and Throughput on CPU/GPU}
\label{sec:latency_throughput}

Runtime was benchmarked on an Intel Core Ultra 9 285K CPU and NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU using the selected seed-1234 binary models. Each variant used 10 warm-up runs and 50 timed repetitions, with variant order randomized within each repetition. The benchmark measured feature extraction, normalization and host-to-device transfer with model inference, and total per-window processing.

Table~\ref{tab:runtime_latency} summarizes the measured latencies and processing rates. For PCC sensing, feature extraction requires 22.30~ms, while normalization, transfer, and inference take 0.78~ms. Total processing takes 23.03~ms, corresponding to 43.42 windows/s. Motor processing takes 67.81~ms, or 14.75 windows/s. Feature extraction therefore dominates runtime for both streams, motivating selective feature computation. Processing time is measured from an available measurement window and excludes acquisition and disk I/O.

\subsection{Feature Pruning for Low-Latency Anomaly Detection}
\label{sec:feature_pruning}

Feature pruning was evaluated for \texttt{fdia2} to reduce online latency. Features were ranked by the decrease in validation balanced accuracy when each feature trajectory was permuted across windows, averaged over three permutations. Reduced subsets used global top-75\%, top-50\%, and top-25\% rankings, as well as per-channel top-15 and top-10 selections. Each subset was retrained using the full model's selected architecture and training protocol. Test labels were not used for ranking. Selective computation extracts only retained features and their required intermediate quantities, so measured runtime reflects the computational benefit of pruning.

Table~\ref{tab:feature_pruning_anomaly2_runtime} shows that pruning reduces latency while approximately preserving PCC detection performance. The PCC top-25\% subset retains 18 of 74 features, maintains 80.29\% balanced accuracy, and reduces processing time from 23.03 to 12.44~ms, giving an 85.18\% speedup. For motor sensing, the top-25\% subset retains 56 of 222 features and reduces latency from 67.81 to 27.87~ms, giving a 143.33\% speedup, with balanced accuracy decreasing from 88.26\% to 86.83\%.

The speedup depends on which calculations remain necessary, as well as the feature count. For example, a retained spectral feature may still require an FFT, even when other spectral statistics are omitted. Fig.~\ref{fig:top10_feature_importance} shows the ten highest-ranked features for each stream, using mean validation importance and one-standard-deviation error bars across the three holdouts. Motor channels 1--3 and 4--6 correspond to ACIM and PMSM currents; PCC channels 1 and 2 correspond to voltage and current. Overall, the pruning results support a compact PCC representation that reduces computation while retaining attack-detection performance.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Why Adaptation Helps More in One Direction}
\label{sec:adaptation_asymmetry}

The adaptation results show that ACIM and PMSM do not form a symmetric transfer pair. PMSM $\rightarrow$ ACIM adaptation is more effective than ACIM $\rightarrow$ PMSM, particularly for PCC sensing. The improvement with the 1\% budget shows that the source-trained detector can be adjusted using a small amount of target data in the favorable direction. Differences in machine dynamics and operating conditions may contribute to this asymmetry, but their individual effects were not isolated. For deployment, the source motor type and the availability of labeled target episodes therefore matter.

\subsection{PCC vs Motor Tradeoffs}
\label{sec:pcc_vs_motor}

The results show a tradeoff between sensing convenience and class separation. Motor-terminal measurements provide stronger detection and severity discrimination on the tested platform. PCC sensing avoids dedicated terminal instrumentation at each motor and uses the shared electrical interface, making it attractive for monitoring heterogeneous drive installations. Its lower severity resolution limits exact bias classification, but the binary detector and severity-conditioned attack recall show that useful attack information remains available.

The PCC LF+Time representation and pruned feature subsets further reduce processing cost with similar binary detection performance. Thus, the value of PCC monitoring lies in combining a shared sensing location with efficient detection, while motor-terminal measurements remain useful when finer severity discrimination is required. Extension to larger installations requires validation beyond the two-branch platform.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The study considers additive, piecewise-constant current-feedback bias on one ACIM/PMSM platform, with trusted monitoring sensors. Three fixed speeds and three load settings were tested for each motor; other attack families, dynamic speed/load changes, converter topologies, and installations were not evaluated. Brake excitation identifies the load settings, while calibrated shaft torque and mechanical harm were not measured. The severity labels therefore describe injected bias magnitude.

Complete-recording holdouts prevent recording and episode overlap within each repeat, but the three repeats reuse data from the same apparatus. Adaptation requires labeled target episodes, and its gains depend on transfer direction. PCC severity errors and false alarms remain limitations for practical use. Finally, runtime measurements use a GPU-assisted workstation; embedded implementation and closed-loop mitigation remain future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper experimentally evaluated nonintrusive FDIA detection and severity assessment on a heterogeneous ACIM/PMSM inverter-fed drive platform. Separate motor-terminal and PCC models were assessed using disjoint recordings and attack episodes. Motor-terminal measurements provided stronger class separation, while PCC measurements supported useful detection without dedicated sensors at each motor. This shared sensing arrangement offers a path toward scalable monitoring, subject to validation on larger installations.

Cross-motor experiments showed that direct transfer is unreliable and that limited-label adaptation can substantially improve detection. In the PMSM-to-ACIM direction, a nominal 1\% target-episode budget improved PCC balanced accuracy by 21.43 percentage points. Feature pruning retained the full PCC model's mean balanced accuracy while reducing processing time to 12.44~ms. These findings support PCC monitoring as a practical sensing option for the tested additive current-feedback attacks, with severity resolution, false alarms, and transfer-direction dependence defining its current limits.

\subsection{Severity-Conditioned Attack Detection}
\label{app:severity_detection}
Table~\ref{tab:severity_detection_recall} separates correct bias-bin classification from detecting any attack. For confusion matrix $C$, severity-conditioned attack recall is
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{attack}|k}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{3}C_{kj}}{\sum_{j=0}^{3}C_{kj}},\qquad k\in\{1,2,3\},
\label{eq:severity_conditioned_detection}
\end{equation}
while exact-severity recall is $C_{kk}/\sum_j C_{kj}$. The repeated false-alarm entries belong to the same four-class model and do not depend on the actual attack level. Fig.~\ref{fig:severity_detection_decisions} presents the corresponding PCC decision matrices; pooled normal recall can differ slightly from the mean per-repeat recall because normal-window counts vary across holdouts.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}
\label{app:sensitivity}
One-factor sensitivity experiments changed depth to 3/9, dropout to 0.1/0.4, or learning rate to $3\times10^{-4}/3\times10^{-3}$ around each selected configuration, retaining all other settings. Fig.~\ref{fig:hyperparameter_sensitivity} reports these perturbations. PCC binary mean balanced accuracy ranges from 72.23\% to 78.33\% among these variants, compared with 80.29\% for the selected reference. PCC severity varies from 51.25\% to 55.71\%, compared with 55.62\%. Motor severity is more sensitive to the high learning rate, falling to 77.69\% mean balanced accuracy. The PCC severity limitation persists across the evaluated settings, although classification performance remains sensitive to the training parameters.

\subsection{Auxiliary Diagnostic Tasks}
\label{app:auxiliary_diagnostics}
The auxiliary motor-type classifier (\texttt{motor2}) distinguishes ACIM and PMSM recording conditions using a two-class softmax output. Its results are included in Table~\ref{tab:all_tasks_in_domain}. For attack-only regression, the target is $100\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_\infty$, in percentage points of nominal current-feedback bias. A scalar output replaces the classification head; training uses Smooth-L1 loss with transition parameter 0.5 and validation mean absolute error (MAE) for checkpoint selection. The severity-selected architecture is retained within each stream and repeat. Motor-side MAE is $0.0196\pm0.0041$ percentage points, with mean $R^2=0.961$; PCC MAE is $0.0981\pm0.0049$ percentage points, with mean $R^2=0.267$. Fig.~\ref{fig:regression_diagnostics} shows prediction distributions at every tested bias level for each repeat. These are estimates of injected amplitude on attacked windows, not measured mechanical impact.
