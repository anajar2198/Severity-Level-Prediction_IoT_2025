\begin{abstract}
False-data-injection attacks on current feedback can compromise the operation of inverter-fed motor drives. In heterogeneous multi-motor systems, installing monitoring sensors at each motor increases instrumentation requirements, while measurements at the shared point of common coupling (PCC) combine responses from different drives. This aggregation makes it difficult to detect small attacks, identify the affected motor, and distinguish injected-bias levels, limiting the use of centralized monitoring.
This paper addresses these tasks using PCC voltage and current measurements, with motor-terminal currents evaluated separately as a reference. Multiscale electrical features and task-specific InceptionTime classifiers are validated on a platform containing an alternating current induction motor and a permanent magnet synchronous motor. The evaluation keeps complete recordings and their episodes separate across training, validation, and test sets and examines cross-motor adaptation and selective feature computation.
PCC measurements achieve mean accuracies of 80.78\% for attack detection and 74.32\% for joint detection and motor localization, supporting monitoring without dedicated motor-terminal sensors. Severity classification remains more challenging, with 61.00\% accuracy. Target-domain fine-tuning improves cross-motor transfer, while feature pruning increases the processing rate by 85.18\% at 80.29\% balanced detection accuracy. These results establish the feasibility and current limitations of PCC monitoring on the tested heterogeneous drive platform.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{F}{alse}-data-injection attack (FDIA) detection and severity assessment are important in networked motor-drive systems, where manipulated sensor feedback can degrade control performance and cause unsafe operation~\cite{Ghaderi2021A}. Increasing drive connectivity makes timely detection of sensor-oriented cyberattacks essential for resilient operation~\cite{Yang2022Fast,Yang2024Enhanced}.
Current signals are attractive for FDIA monitoring because they are easy to acquire, strongly coupled to electromechanical dynamics, and have been shown to reveal sensor-integrity attacks in motor drives~\cite{7944701, YangVulnerability}. However, most existing studies emphasize terminal-side sensing, operate on a single machine domain, or assume sufficient labeled data for supervised training. These assumptions limit practicality in heterogeneous deployments where different motor types, sensing locations, and data availability constraints all affect performance.
Motivated by these limitations, this work experimentally evaluates FDIA detection in a heterogeneous motor-drive setting involving both alternating current induction motor (ACIM) and permanent magnet synchronous motor (PMSM) domains. The study evaluates binary detection (\texttt{fdia2}), four-level severity assessment (\texttt{severity4}), cross-domain transfer, and deployment feasibility. The framework combines low-frequency (LF), high-frequency (HF), and time-domain (TD) electrical features with an InceptionTime classifier, and compares motor-terminal and point of common coupling (PCC) sensing. A three-class classifier (\texttt{fdia3}) additionally distinguishes normal operation, an ACIM attack, and a PMSM attack.

\subsection{Challenges at PCC in Heterogeneous Multi-Motor Systems}
\label{sec:pcc_challenges}

From a deployment perspective, motor-terminal measurements provide cleaner machine-specific signatures, whereas PCC measurements are more scalable and nonintrusive. PCC sensing is attractive because it avoids direct motor-terminal instrumentation and is compatible with shared electrical infrastructure~\cite{7271086}. However, PCC currents are affected by converter harmonics, network interactions, and load aggregation. Consequently, PCC attack signatures are weaker and less separable than motor-terminal signatures~\cite{10005067}.
The problem becomes more difficult in heterogeneous multi-motor settings. ACIM and PMSM systems exhibit different current characteristics even under the same attack framework, which introduces a substantial domain shift between source and target deployments. Consequently, a detector trained on one motor family may generalize poorly to the other unless explicit adaptation is used.
\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
The main contributions of this work are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Nonintrusive FDIA monitoring in heterogeneous motor drives:} We develop and experimentally validate a PCC monitoring framework that combines LF, HF, and TD electrical features with task-specific InceptionTime classifiers, enabling attack detection, localization to the ACIM or PMSM, and injected-bias severity assessment without dedicated motor-terminal sensors.
\item \textbf{Cross-motor adaptation with limited labeled data:} We evaluate a supervised adaptation strategy that fine-tunes a source-trained detector using complete labeled target-domain episodes, enabling a PCC balanced-accuracy gain of 21.43 percentage points for PMSM-to-ACIM transfer with a nominal 1\% target-episode budget.
\item \textbf{Computationally efficient PCC monitoring:} We develop a reduced-feature inference pipeline that selects features using validation-set permutation importance and computes only the selected features and their required intermediate quantities, enabling an 85.18\% processing speedup with 18 of 74 features while retaining 80.29\% mean detection balanced accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedWorks}

FDIA detection has been studied extensively in smart grids and broader cyber-physical systems (CPS), but the assumptions in that literature do not transfer cleanly to inverter-fed motor drives. Many grid- and CPS-oriented approaches rely on state estimation, observer-based detection, invariant analysis, adaptive neuro-fuzzy models, or graph-based learning~\cite{7438916,basati2022fdi,nath2022quickest,beg2017fdia,sargolzaei2020fdia,10016905}. These studies demonstrate strong FDIA-detection capability at the system level, but they are generally not designed for drive-level current monitoring or for deployment at a shared PCC.

Motor-drive-specific studies have shown that current signals can support effective FDIA detection in electric-drive systems and that transfer learning can improve performance when labeled data are limited~\cite{Yang2022Fast,Yang2024Enhanced}. Related industrial-IoT monitoring studies have also considered cyber-detection in machine-monitoring settings~\cite{423563247547}. However, these studies mainly rely on motor-terminal sensing, single-motor settings, or closely related operating domains, and therefore provide limited evidence for heterogeneous cross-motor transfer.





The main gap is not FDIA detection in general, which has been widely studied in smart grids and cyber-physical systems, but nonintrusive PCC-based monitoring for heterogeneous inverter-fed motor-drive systems. In particular, the existing literature does not clearly establish: 1) how PCC sensing compares with motor-side sensing for low-severity current-feedback FDIAs, 2) whether PCC measurements support reliable multi-level severity assessment rather than only binary detection, given that attack signatures become weaker and less separable under signal aggregation and harmonic distortion, and 3) how detection performance transfers between heterogeneous ACIM and PMSM domains when labeled target-domain data are limited. This work addresses these three questions experimentally on a shared ACIM/PMSM platform using multi-scale features, in-domain evaluation, source-only cross-domain transfer, and few-shot target-domain adaptation.

\section{System and Data}

\label{sec:system_data}

The experimental platform consists of a heterogeneous two-motor industrial setup including one ACIM branch and one PMSM branch. Both branches are supplied from a common upstream electrical interface, enabling measurements at the PCC in addition to motor-side measurements. This setup enables comparison of motor-terminal and PCC sensing under heterogeneous ACIM/PMSM dynamics.
\subsection{Experimental Platform: ACIM/PMSM and PCC Sensing}
\label{sec:experimental_platform}

Fig.~\ref{fig:system_schematic} illustrates the overall setup, including the shared PCC, the two drive branches, the ACIM and PMSM loads, the sensing locations, and the FDIA injection points in the control/feedback paths.
The dataset contains 540 scheduled attack episodes (270 for each motor), formed by repeating 30 bias patterns over nine speed/load combinations per motor. Both motors operated throughout: only the ACIM was attacked in ACIM-labeled recordings, and only the PMSM was attacked in PMSM-labeled recordings. Both motors were attack-free during normal intervals. The experiments cover three speed operating points, namely 900, 1100, and 1300 RPM.

Two measurement streams were used throughout this study. The first stream corresponds to motor-side measurements, referred to as \emph{motor} in the remainder of the paper. The second stream corresponds to PCC-side measurements, referred to as \emph{PCC}.

\subsection{Attack Model, Sensing Assumptions, and Labels}
\label{sec:attack_labels}

In the testbed, the FDIA is injected into the controller's phase-current feedback path. The PCC and motor-side monitoring sensors are used as trusted measurements to record the physical response of the drive system under attack.
The monitoring sensors are isolated from the compromised controller feedback path. Thus, PCC sensing represents the nonintrusive monitoring case, while motor-side sensing serves as a performance reference. Replay, denial-of-service, monitoring-sensor attacks, and malicious command manipulation are outside the scope of this study.

Let $\mathbf{i}_{abc}(t)=[i_a(t),i_b(t),i_c(t)]^\top$ denote the true three-phase current-feedback vector in the controller's per-unit representation, and let $\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_{abc}(t)$ denote the corrupted current-feedback vector received by the motor controller. During an attack interval, the FDIA is modeled as an additive phase-current bias:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_{abc}(t)
=
\mathbf{i}_{abc}(t)+\boldsymbol{\alpha},
\qquad t\in[t_{\mathrm{on}},t_{\mathrm{off}}],
\label{eq:fdia_model}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\alpha_a,\alpha_b,\alpha_c]^\top\) denotes the injected per-unit bias vector, with \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{0}\) outside the attack interval. The tested nonzero bias magnitudes range from \(0.0005\) to \(0.0050~\mathrm{pu}\), corresponding to \(0.05\%\)--\(0.5\%\) current-feedback bias. These low-severity FDIAs are difficult to detect because their signatures can be masked by normal variation, harmonics, and PCC aggregation. The bias is normalized to the nominal controller current-feedback base. In this study, severity is defined by the injected bias magnitude; physical consequences such as shaft-torque deviation are not used as severity labels. Attack severity is measured by the largest absolute injected phase-current bias,
\(s=\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_\infty\).

For binary FDIA detection, each window is labeled as
$y_{\mathrm{fdia2}}=1$ if $s>0$ and $y_{\mathrm{fdia2}}=0$ otherwise.
For joint detection and localization (\texttt{fdia3}), labels 0, 1, and 2 denote normal operation (33,054 windows), ACIM attack (13,500), and PMSM attack (13,500), respectively. Only one motor is attacked at a time.
For four-class severity assessment, the label is defined as
\begin{equation}
y_{\mathrm{severity4}} =
\begin{cases}
0, & s=0 \quad \text{(normal)},\\
1, & 0.0005 \leq s \leq 0.0015 \quad \text{(low)},\\
2, & 0.0020 \leq s \leq 0.0035 \quad \text{(medium)},\\
3, & 0.0040 \leq s \leq 0.0050 \quad \text{(high)}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:severity_bins}
\end{equation}
Cases 21--30 have $s=0.0050$ throughout because phase A remains fixed; all belong to the high-bias class. The resulting attack-window counts are 5,400 low, 7,200 medium, and 14,400 high.
Table~\ref{tab:cyberattack_dataset_structure} summarizes the swept single-phase, equal two-phase, and fixed-plus-swept two-phase FDIA cases. Each recording contains 30 consecutive 20-s episodes, with 10~s normal operation followed by 10~s FDIA operation. In total, the dataset contains 33,054 normal and 27,000 attack windows.

\subsection{Data Acquisition and Operating Conditions}
\label{sec:data_acquisition}

Electrical measurements were acquired from the testbed using a National Instruments cDAQ-9185 chassis and a custom data-collector interface board. The interface board routed synchronized motor-side and PCC-side analog channels to the acquisition system. Table~\ref{tab:measurement_streams} summarizes the measured signals and channel counts. Three brake-excitation settings were tested for each machine: 1--3~V for the ACIM branch and 2--4~V for the PMSM branch. Because torque was not directly measured, these brake-excitation voltages are used as load-condition labels. All channels were sampled at 10~kHz and stored in TDMS format. For learning-based analysis, each record was segmented into non-overlapping 0.2~s windows containing 2{,}000 samples. To prevent temporal leakage, complete recordings and all their episodes were assigned to separate training, validation, and test sets. Normalization statistics were fitted using training recordings only. The resulting dataset dimensions, input configurations, severity-label distributions, and evaluation protocols are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_protocols}. Three seeds (1234, 2234, and 3234) changed the recording assignment and model initialization; recordings can recur across repeats.

\section{The Proposed Method}
\label{sec:method}

To represent both slow electromechanical behavior and faster attack-induced disturbances, a two-scale LF/HF feature pipeline was applied independently to each measured channel. Separate classifiers use these features for attack detection, motor localization, and injected-bias severity assessment.

\subsection{LF/HF Feature Extraction Pipeline}
\label{sec:lfhf_features}

For every sample, a 2{,}000-point outer window was first extracted from the raw 10~kHz recording. Within this outer window, two parallel sliding analyses were performed. The LF branch uses a 500-sample window with a stride of 25 samples, while the HF branch uses a 250-sample window with a stride of 29 samples. The LF and HF settings both produce 61 time steps, enabling concatenation into one temporal feature tensor.
Fig.~\ref{fig:lfhf_motor_pcc_examples} illustrates representative attack-onset responses and the corresponding LF/HF windows. Signal amplitudes are in recorded acquisition units.


In total, 37 features were extracted per channel, including 18 LF spectral features, 11 HF spectral/envelope features, and 8 TD features. The LF branch targets slow electromechanical changes, fundamental-frequency behavior, harmonic distortion, sideband effects, and low-band energy redistribution. The HF branch captures higher-frequency redistribution and envelope-related changes that may appear during small current-feedback FDIAs. The TD features provide complementary sensitivity to amplitude variation, impulsiveness, waveform asymmetry, and short-term signal complexity.
Together, these features help make weak PCC FDIA signatures more separable despite harmonics, grid interaction, and aggregation.

\subsection{Inception-Based Detection Model}
\label{sec:inception_model}

The extracted LF/HF tensors were modeled using a one-dimensional InceptionTime architecture~\cite{fawaz2020inceptiontime} for multivariate time-series classification. The input dimension is $(61,222)$ for the motor stream and $(61,74)$ for the PCC stream. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:inceptiontime_architecture}, the network uses bottleneck compression, parallel one-dimensional convolutional kernels of lengths 39, 19, and 9, residual shortcuts, global average pooling, and a dense output layer. Each task uses a separately trained model with two outputs for \texttt{fdia2}, three for \texttt{fdia3}, and four for \texttt{severity4}.

\subsection{Architecture of Online FDIA Detection and Attack-Severity Estimation}
\label{sec:AnomalyDetection}

The proposed pipeline supports online monitoring from trusted motor-terminal currents or PCC voltage and current measurements. Fig.~\ref{fig:Online-algorithem} illustrates the overall architecture of the proposed monitoring pipeline. In the deployed setting, fixed-length windows of measurement data are acquired from either the motor terminals or the PCC, passed through the LF/HF/TD feature-extraction stage, and then evaluated by the selected learning model to produce anomaly or severity decisions. The measurement, feature-extraction, and inference stages are modular, allowing the same pipeline to operate with motor-side or PCC inputs and supporting feature pruning, reduced-input models, and cross-motor adaptation. The framework provides alarms, severity indicators, and logs for supervisory systems; automatic mitigation is outside the scope. The same architecture is used for binary detection and severity classification, with separate normalization and trained models for motor-side and PCC inputs.

\subsection{Training Details and Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:training_details}

For the InceptionTime-based models, hyperparameter selection was performed separately for \texttt{fdia2} and \texttt{severity4} in the motor and PCC streams using validation-set balanced accuracy. The search varied depth (3/4/6), filters (16/24/32/48), bottleneck width (8/16/32), dropout (0.20/0.35/0.50), learning rate ($5\times10^{-4}$/$10^{-3}$), weight decay ($5\times10^{-5}$/$10^{-4}$), and batch size (64/128). Table~\ref{tab:search_top5_combined} summarizes the top-ranked configurations for both sensing streams across all three seeds.

The same 100 candidate configurations were evaluated for two tasks, two sensing streams, and three recording partitions, totaling 1,200 training runs.

Training used Adam and weighted cross-entropy, assigning larger weights to less frequent classes. Each run used up to 60 epochs, with early stopping after 10 epochs without improved validation balanced accuracy. The learning rate was halved after three unimproved epochs, down to $10^{-6}$. The best checkpoint and configuration were selected independently within each recording partition using validation balanced accuracy; ties followed the fixed candidate order. Test results were not used for selection. The \texttt{fdia3} model retained the \texttt{severity4}-selected configuration for each stream and holdout and was trained from fresh weights with three outputs. Its checkpoint was selected by three-class validation balanced accuracy.

Reported test metrics are mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation over the three holdouts. Precision, recall, and F1 are macro-averaged, and balanced accuracy is the mean class recall. The standard deviations describe variation across recording assignments and model initializations, rather than independent physical replications.

\subsection{Domain Adaptation Strategy: Source-Only vs Few-Shot Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:domain_adaptation}

Few-shot adaptation was evaluated by training on recordings targeting one motor and testing on recordings targeting the other. Two transfer directions were considered: ACIM $\rightarrow$ PMSM and PMSM $\rightarrow$ ACIM. For each direction, the detector was first trained using labeled source recordings and then evaluated directly on the target recordings. This \emph{source-only} setting quantifies cross-motor domain shift. Six source recordings were used for training and three for validation, with the 100-candidate search selected by source validation balanced accuracy.

For adaptation, the source-trained model was fine-tuned using a small labeled subset of complete target-domain episode groups before testing on separate target recordings. The nominal target adaptation ratios considered in this work were 1\%, 5\%, 10\%, and 20\% of the available target-domain adaptation pool (Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_protocols}); the 1\% budget selects two of 180 groups (1.11\%). Fine-tuning used 20 epochs at 0.2 times the source learning rate, with source normalization fixed.
The domain-adaptation strategy was implemented as supervised fine-tuning rather than unsupervised feature alignment. To ensure reliable comparison, all cross-domain experiments were repeated over three random seeds.

The \texttt{fdia3} rows report joint detection and localization, including the normal class. Row percentages are pooled over the three holdouts.



Exact-severity recall counts attacks assigned to the correct bias class. Severity-conditioned attack recall also counts attacks assigned to another nonzero bias class. Fig.~\ref{fig:severity_detection_decisions}(c), (d) presents this second view of the PCC severity model: it detects 88.1\% of high-bias attacks as some attack level, compared with 70.8\% assigned to the exact high-bias class. Thus, an incorrect severity estimate can still provide an attack indication. The same model labels 37.3\% of normal windows as attacks, so this detection benefit must be considered together with false alarms. These decisions come from the four-class model; the separately trained binary detector in Fig.~\ref{fig:main_confusion_matrices}(b) has fewer false alarms.

For \texttt{severity4}, XGBoost achieved higher accuracy and LSTM-FCN slightly higher macro F1. The proposed model therefore improves average class recall for PCC severity assessment, while the preferred model depends on the evaluation metric.

\subsection{Domain Adaptation Results: 1/5/10/20\%}
\label{sec:adaptation_results}

Table~\ref{tab:domain_adaptation_anomaly2} reports \texttt{fdia2} few-shot adaptation using nominal budgets of 1\%, 5\%, 10\%, and 20\% labeled target episode groups. At the 20\% budget, adaptation improved performance relative to the source-only setting in all cases, although the magnitude of the gain was strongly direction dependent. For ACIM-to-PMSM transfer, adaptation reaches 75.99\% balanced accuracy for motor sensing and 56.30\% for PCC at 20\% target data. For PMSM-to-ACIM transfer, adaptation is much stronger, reaching 92.63\% balanced accuracy for motor sensing and 91.38\% for PCC at 20\% target data.
With only the nominal 1\% budget, PMSM-to-ACIM balanced accuracy rises from 36.08\% to 64.35\% for motor sensing and from 56.00\% to 77.43\% for PCC sensing, gains of 28.27 and 21.43 percentage points.

Overall, the adaptation results show that few-shot target-domain fine-tuning can improve transfer performance, but the gains are strongly direction dependent and are much larger in the PMSM $\rightarrow$ ACIM direction. This asymmetry indicates that cross-motor FDIA adaptation cannot be treated as a symmetric transfer problem; in practice, the pretraining motor domain materially affects PCC deployment performance.

\subsection{Error Analysis: Confusion Matrices and FP/FN Behavior}
\label{sec:error_analysis}

Fig.~\ref{fig:main_confusion_matrices} compares the in-domain confusion matrices for \texttt{fdia2} and \texttt{fdia3}. For \texttt{fdia2}, motor-side sensing yields a strongly diagonal confusion matrix for attack windows, with pooled false-positive (FP) and false-negative (FN) rates of 20.9\% and 2.3\%, respectively. PCC sensing yields 14.7\% false positives and 24.6\% false negatives, showing fewer false alarms but more missed attacks. The \texttt{fdia3} matrices show that false alarms and missed attacks are more frequent than confusion between the attacked motor types. Mean normal false-alarm rates are 22.85\% for motor sensing and 33.12\% for PCC sensing. For \texttt{severity4} in Fig.~\ref{fig:severity_detection_decisions}(a), (b), PCC errors include both normal/attack confusion and confusion among the nonzero bias classes, indicating that PCC measurements retain coarse attack information but blur finer severity distinctions.

Fig.~\ref{fig:case1_30_motor_pcc_detection_examples} complements the aggregate confusion-matrix results by showing a representative held-out ACIM detection sequence on a continuous experimental signal. The motor-side detector follows most scheduled normal/FDIA intervals, while the PCC-side detector exhibits missed attacks and false alarms, especially near the first low-bias attack. These results indicate that motor-side measurements are more separable, while PCC measurements remain useful but less reliable for detecting small attacks. Root-mean-square (RMS) traces use 500-sample frames at stride 25, and predictions are placed at window ends. Green curves show attack probability, red curves show predicted labels, and dashed gray curves show true labels; the dotted threshold is 0.5.

\section{LATENCY AND REDUCED-FEATURE INFERENCE ANALYSIS}
\label{sec:realtime}

Practical PCC monitoring requires low latency and stable online throughput. This section reports per-window latency, throughput, pruning, and reduced-feature effects.

\subsection{Inference Latency and Throughput on CPU/GPU}
\label{sec:latency_throughput}

Runtime was benchmarked on an Intel Core Ultra 9 285K CPU and NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU using the selected seed-1234 LF/HF-Inception binary pipelines. The benchmark was split into three stages: (i) LF/HF feature extraction from one 2{,}000-sample raw window, (ii) normalization, host-to-device transfer, and model inference on the resulting feature tensor, and (iii) end-to-end per-window execution. Each variant used 10 warm-up runs and 50 timed repetitions, with randomized variant order. Processing starts from an available measurement window; acquisition and disk I/O are excluded.

Table~\ref{tab:runtime_latency} shows that feature extraction dominates processing time. Median end-to-end latency is 23.03~ms for PCC and 67.81~ms for motor sensing.

\subsection{Feature Pruning for Low-Latency Anomaly Detection}
\label{sec:feature_pruning}

Feature pruning was evaluated for the \texttt{fdia2} task to reduce online latency. Features were ranked by permutation importance, defined as the validation balanced-accuracy drop caused by permuting each feature trajectory across windows, averaged over three permutations. Test labels were not used for ranking. Reduced subsets were then formed using global top-\(75\%\), top-\(50\%\), and top-\(25\%\) rankings, as well as per-channel top-15 and top-10 selections. Because the benchmark uses selective LF/HF feature computation, only retained features and their required intermediate quantities are calculated; therefore, the measured extraction latency reflects the computational benefit of pruning. Each reduced subset was retrained using the full model's selected architecture and training protocol.


Table~\ref{tab:feature_pruning_anomaly2_runtime} shows that pruning can reduce latency while preserving, and in some cases improving, detection performance. For the PCC stream, a compact representation was obtained with the top-\(25\%\) subset, which retained 18 of 74 features. This subset retained the full model's mean balanced accuracy of 80.29\% and reduced end-to-end CPU+GPU latency from 23.03~ms to 12.44~ms, corresponding to an 85.18\% speedup. For the motor stream, the top-\(25\%\) subset retained 56 of 222 features, reduced balanced accuracy from 88.26\% to 86.83\%, and reduced latency from 67.81~ms to 27.87~ms, corresponding to a 143.33\% speedup.

All evaluated subsets reduced runtime, but the reduction was not proportional to feature count. This indicates that pruning effectiveness depends not only on feature count, but also on which LF, HF, and TD calculations remain necessary. The feature-importance rankings in Fig.~\ref{fig:top10_feature_importance} further show that the two sensing streams rely on different features: motor-side detection is dominated by TD and selected LF features, whereas PCC-side detection places greater emphasis on harmonic and waveform-shape features. Bars show mean validation importance and whiskers show one standard deviation across three holdouts. Motor channels 1--3/4--6 are ACIM/PMSM currents; PCC channels 1/2 are voltage/current.

\subsection{LF/HF/TD Ablation for Binary Anomaly Detection}

LF+Time nearly matches the full PCC model, while time-only and HF+Time outperform the full motor model (Table~\ref{tab:lfhf_ablation_anomaly2}). \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results reveal a consistent tradeoff between sensing convenience and predictive separability. The adaptation results show that ACIM and PMSM do not form a symmetric transfer pair.
\subsection{Why Adaptation Helps More in One Direction}
\label{sec:adaptation_asymmetry}

PMSM $\rightarrow$ ACIM adaptation is much more effective than ACIM $\rightarrow$ PMSM adaptation, particularly for PCC sensing. In the PCC stream, Table~\ref{tab:domain_adaptation_anomaly2} shows a substantial PMSM $\rightarrow$ ACIM gain even at the nominal 1\% budget, while the reverse direction remains less effective. These results show that the benefit of few-shot fine-tuning depends on the source and target motor types; the physical causes of the asymmetry were not isolated. For practical PCC deployment, a small amount of target-domain calibration data is therefore important when monitoring attacks on a motor family absent from the source attack examples.

\subsection{PCC vs Motor Tradeoffs}
\label{sec:pcc_vs_motor}



PCC remains the more deployment-oriented option because it avoids direct terminal access and matches shared industrial power infrastructure. A shared PCC sensing point also avoids dedicated instrumentation at each motor, offering a path toward scalable monitoring; larger installations remain to be validated. Despite lower separability, PCC achieved useful in-domain detection and strong adapted performance, especially for PMSM $\rightarrow$ ACIM transfer.



\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations should be noted when interpreting the present results. First, the study considers only two motor families, ACIM and PMSM, under additive current-feedback bias on one experimental platform. The recordings use fixed speed/load settings; dynamic changes and converter topologies were not varied. The three recording holdouts reuse this apparatus and are not independent hardware replications. Localization was evaluated with one attacked branch at a time; simultaneous attacks on both motors were not tested. Because the attacked branch is fixed within each recording, operating-condition cues may also contribute to localization.
Broader validation across additional machines, loads, control modes, sensing setups, and attack families is needed before generalizing beyond the tested platform.

Second, the domain-adaptation analysis is based on supervised few-shot fine-tuning with labeled target-domain data. This does not cover cases where target labels are unavailable or delayed. Unsupervised or self-supervised adaptation strategies may therefore be required for more constrained deployment scenarios.

Third, the deployment analysis was performed using a GPU-assisted software implementation and focused mainly on the binary FDIA detection task; embedded implementation and task-specific optimization for severity assessment remain future work.

Finally, PCC severity classification and false alarms remain limitations. Some severity baselines achieved higher accuracy or macro F1; the proposed model's severity advantage is in mean balanced accuracy, not universal model dominance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper experimentally evaluated PCC-based detection of low-severity current-feedback FDIAs in a heterogeneous ACIM/PMSM inverter-fed drive platform. Motor-terminal measurements provided the strongest separability, but PCC measurements supported useful nonintrusive detection despite aggregation, harmonics, and grid-side interactions. In the mixed-domain setting with separate recordings and episodes, the proposed LF/HF-InceptionTime method achieved useful \texttt{fdia2} detection from both sensing locations, while PCC \texttt{severity4} estimates were less reliable and should be interpreted as coarse diagnostics. Cross-motor experiments showed substantial ACIM/PMSM domain shift, making source-only transfer unreliable. Few-shot target-domain fine-tuning improved transfer performance, with much larger gains for PMSM $\rightarrow$ ACIM adaptation than for ACIM $\rightarrow$ PMSM adaptation. A nominal 1\% target-episode budget improved PMSM-to-ACIM PCC balanced accuracy by 21.43 percentage points. Runtime and pruning results further indicate that the PCC pipeline is compatible with the required online processing rate. Overall, the results indicate that PCC-based FDIA monitoring is promising on the tested ACIM/PMSM platform under additive current-feedback attacks.

\subsection{Severity-Conditioned Attack Detection}
\label{app:severity_detection}
Table~\ref{tab:severity_detection_recall} separates correct bias-bin classification from detecting any attack. For confusion matrix $C$, severity-conditioned attack recall is
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{attack}|k}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{3}C_{kj}}{\sum_{j=0}^{3}C_{kj}},\qquad k\in\{1,2,3\},
\label{eq:severity_conditioned_detection}
\end{equation}
while exact-severity recall is $C_{kk}/\sum_j C_{kj}$. The repeated false-alarm entries belong to the same four-class model and do not depend on the actual attack level. Fig.~\ref{fig:severity_detection_decisions}(c), (d) presents the corresponding PCC decision matrices; pooled normal recall can differ slightly from the mean per-repeat recall because normal-window counts vary across holdouts.
